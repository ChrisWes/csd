Research conducted by Vanson Bourne on behalf of NashTech, 2025. N=1,000.

The decision to build custom software is almost never ideological. Organisations with predominantly bought tech stacks reach specific points where what is available cannot do what the business needs.

Integration is the most common trigger. 44% of organisations chose custom specifically because it offered better fit with their existing systems than any available COTS solution.

Close behind it: the need for roadmap control, better user experience, and security or data sovereignty requirements. These are not abstract preferences. They are the points at which a bought solution stops being fit for purpose.

The integration driver runs across every sector we surveyed — strongest in IT and technology (61%), but present in manufacturing, consumer goods, and logistics at comparable levels. The friction is not sector-specific. The systems causing it are.

There is a structural tension at the heart of custom software delivery that the survey data makes visible with unusual clarity.

The single most common reason organisations choose to build custom is better integration with existing systems. The single most common reason those projects run into difficulty is integration complexity.

The same force is driving demand and throttling delivery.

The implication reaches beyond current delivery timelines. Legacy integration architecture is already the primary constraint on AI adoption for 43% of organisations surveyed. Custom software built on fragile integration foundations does not scale into an AI-native operating environment.

Integration is not a technical problem that sits beneath the strategy. For most organisations, it is the strategy — and it is being treated like plumbing.

Senior leaders and the teams delivering their custom software programmes are not experiencing the same projects.

Board members and C-suite executives report strong outcomes against business cases. 63% say their projects exceeded expectations. Mid-level managers and those closest to daily delivery report a different picture. Only 39% say the same.

The 24-point gap between those two numbers is where return on investment quietly leaks.

The gap is not a mystery. Delivery managers are dealing with scope creep, integration friction, and workarounds that never appear in the business case summary presented upward.

The problem is rarely lack of ambition. It is that success is being measured differently at every level of the organisation — and the discrepancy is not being surfaced.

Most organisations using third-party software development partners describe that relationship as a trusted delivery arrangement — consistent, reliable, repeatable. 47% sit in this category.

32% have something more: a partner closely aligned with their long-term goals, involved in high-value strategic initiatives, with shared accountability for outcomes rather than outputs.

97% of respondents say they would invest more in a partner who consistently delivers long-term value. The gap between that aspiration and the 32% who currently have a strategic relationship is not a sourcing problem. It is a relationship architecture problem — and often a reflection of what was specified in the original procurement.

92% of organisations use offshore partners for custom software development. 99% of them report measurably better outcomes. The operating model — how the relationship is structured, governed, and maintained — determines which side of that equation you land on.
